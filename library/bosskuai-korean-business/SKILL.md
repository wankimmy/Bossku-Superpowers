# BosskuAI Korean Business

Use this skill to prepare for dealing with Korean counterparts: pace the deal to how approvals actually happen, and write messages that fit the relationship stage.

## How this differs from nearby skills

- **`bosskuai-sales-strategy`**: general pipeline and objection handling. This skill adds Korean-specific pacing and etiquette.
- **`bosskuai-i18n-l10n`**: localising a product. This skill is about people and communication.
- **`bosskuai-lead-intelligence`**: researching and ranking leads.
- **`bosskuai-visa-doc-translate`**: document translation, not business conduct.
- **`bosskuai-investor-outreach`**: investor contact in general.

## Workflow

1. Assess the relationship: how you were introduced, current stage (introduction, acquaintance, trusted), the contact's title and likely decision power, and which channel they use.
2. Map the company type and the likely approval path. Decisions usually go through an internal review and a written approval document passed up an approval chain (often called pumui or gyeoljae), so expect longer cycles than a single meeting suggests. Treat any quoted durations as rough and confirm with your contact.
3. Pace the first meetings: listen more than you pitch, do not press for a closing date, and leave money until the relationship supports it.
4. Work through your entry-point contact. Do not go over their head to a senior person without their knowledge.
5. Decode indirect replies with care. A phrase like "we will review it" can be a soft no or a genuine process start; judge from the follow-up signals (requests for references, pricing, scope, or internal circulation of your material) and ask a gentle open question such as "which part concerns you?".
6. Write messages by stage. First contact: formal, name the introducer, one line about you, a small ask. Later: semi-formal with a clear reason. Use titles plus the honorific until invited otherwise. In group chats use Korean (or have a native speaker review) and keep English for one-to-one chats where the relationship supports it.
7. Follow up lightly. Silence for several days after a meeting often means internal discussion; avoid a flood of messages. Pick a frequency suited to the company and stage.
8. For dining and drinking events, learn the seating, pouring, and receiving customs, and decline gracefully if you need to. Check dates for holidays (Lunar New Year, Chuseok) and avoid asking for business during them.
9. For a new relationship, consider a small, bounded proof project with a clear deliverable, written so your contact can share it internally.

## Guardrails

- Cultural patterns vary by company, industry, generation, and person. Treat this as a prior to test, not a rule; ask a trusted local contact or interpreter.
- Have Korean text checked by a native speaker before sending anything important. Contracts and pricing need qualified legal and finance review.
- Do not send messages, gifts, or proposals on the user's behalf without their approval.
